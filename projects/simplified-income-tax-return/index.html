Overview

What if the most anxiety-inducing civic obligation could feel as simple as filling out a form?

In a country where only ~2% of the population files taxes, the barrier isn't just awareness. It's the experience itself. The existing system buried salaried filers under 40+ form variants, 750 data fields, and a process that assumed professional assistance as a default.

Problem

The form knew tax codes. It didn't know the taxpayer.

Every taxpayer — salaried employee, pensioner, sole proprietor, corporate director — was handed the same form. Not because the system was poorly maintained, but because it was never designed around the person on the other end. It had no concept of relevance. No logic for what applied to whom.

The result was a service that had quietly outsourced its own usability failure. If you couldn't navigate it alone, you hired someone who could. Practitioner dependency inevitably became the human layer that absorbed all the complexity the system failed to resolve.

Opportunity

A once-in-a-generation chance to rewire tax filing for 12 million people.

This was national scale service design, not a patch on a broken system. Fix the experience for the right people, in the right way, and compliance doesn't just improve. It compounds.

The neglected majority

99.83% of the people the system was built for couldn't use it effectively without help. The biggest lever for tax compliance wasn't enforcement. It was usability.

A deliberate entry point

Salaried persons and pensioners have the most consistent, predictable taxpayer profile in the system. The least ambiguity. The most to gain from self-service. The right place to prove the model before scaling it.

Compliance growth

A self-serviceable tax return doesn't just help existing filers more confidently. It removes the single biggest barrier stopping new ones from starting at all.

Design Goals

Three design goals shaping every decision that followed.

While there were numerous goals and priorities for the redesigned income tax return service, as outlined below.

There were three key service design goals that we intended to achieve through the redesign.

Unification

Over 40 filing options had accumulated over time. Each created to solve a specific problem, unintentionally resulting in larger complexity. We must consolidate them into a single, unified filing experience.

Customisation

The existing system displayed nearly 800 fields to every user, regardless of relevance. The redesign needed to show only what applies to each person's taxpayer profile, income sources, and filing needs.

Structured data architecture

Every field was designed to capture data in a structured, standardised format. We aimed to build a system that's ready to receive future data integrations, pre-filling fields automatically rather than requiring manual entry.

Solution

Simplified Income Tax Return: A guided, personalised filing experience that shows only what applies to you.

A return built around Pakistan's most common taxpayer, aligned with FBR's objectives and the Prime Minister's tax digitalisation initiative.

Core Features

A guided filing journey

With step-by-step navigation, the return now tells you where you are, what's next, and when you're done.

Adaptive form personalisation

You select your income sources. The return builds itself around them.

Scalable & modular
service architecture

Data entered once, structured correctly, usable downstream.

Outcomes

The Simplified Income Tax Return went live in fiscal year 2025. Covered by national media. Lauded as a milestone in Pakistan's tax digitalisation drive. Featured as part of the country's broader economic reform agenda tied to the IMF programme.

Understanding the Domain

You can't redesign a return without understanding the tax laws.

Every section of the redesigned return began with a thorough reading of the relevant sections of the Income Tax Ordinance.

Once we had a working understanding of the legislation, we validated it with the domain experts at FBR. Noting our questions and observations along the way. Only once the requirement and concept was clear and confirmed, did we move to design.

Design Decisions

Simplifying the experience required complicating the thinking.

Every screen hides a problem that had to be solved before it could be designed.

Cracking the Property Schema

The discovery: Revenue leakage by design

Seven different property schemas existed across IRIS. None of them consistent or structured.

FBR couldn't track property values year over year, couldn't link sold properties, couldn't catch undeclared ones. This wasn't just a UX problem. It was causing revenue leakage at a national scale.

The mapping: Making the invisible visible

We pulled every instance of property data entry across IRIS and laid them side by side in Mural. Field by field. Noting down each label, requirement, dependency, and data type.

We carefully reviewed what each schema captured, what conflicted, and what was missing. This exercise turned a vague sense that something was wrong into a precise understanding of what exactly needed to change and why.

The complication: Every answer uncovered a new question.

What began as a schema reconciliation problem quickly became something larger. The deeper we went, the more edge cases, dependencies, and unanswered questions emerged. As our understanding evolved, so did the scope of what we needed to design for.

The system had records. It just couldn't use them.

Property data declared in previous years was unstructured — just strings of text the system couldn't parse. Taxpayers filing their current return needed to see properties they had declared before, but that historical data was essentially useless in its existing form.

The task became not just designing for new data, but figuring out how to surface old data back to the taxpayer and get it cleaned up as part of the filing process. All without overwhelming them in the process.

A new income source that rewrote the problem

While we were working our this problem, the domain team introduced 'Capital Gain from Sale of Property' as an income source. What followed was a cascade of new problems — sold properties, partial sales, cross-year ownership tracking — each requiring its own design solution.

The plot thickened. Pun intended.

The resolution: A unified schema built to last.

One schema. Every field structured. Every edge case solved.

One string to twelve structured data fields

Old data sits at the top as reference. Below it, every property detail captured as a distinct structured field. Better tracking and verification of property ownership.

No more ghost properties

Property sold during the tax year, marked in one step, removed from the wealth statement automatically. What previously had no mechanism now has a clean, consequential interaction built around it.

Not every sale is complete

Partial sales required their own logic. The sold portion is declared and closed. The retained portion carries forward. The taxpayer just marks the percentage, the return does the rest.

Content Design

Writing for taxpayers who don't speak tax

Copy was treated with the same rigour as the screens themselves. Every label, instruction, and tooltip was drafted in multiple versions, reviewed for tone and clarity, and tracked meticulously before making it to the final design.

In Mural, my team lead and I would draft 3-4 versions of the same text string, compare them side by side, and pick the one that felt most natural to a first-time filer. Then we'd walk through the entire journey again to make sure it all sounded like one voice.

Prototyping & Testing

A prototype that worked like the real thing, and a QA process to make sure it did.

Clickable wasn't enough.

Time was ticking. We needed an interactive prototype for user testing. One where income figures carried forward across screens, fed calculations, and populated the wealth statement and withholding tax sections in real time. Figma couldn't do the job. So I picked Justinmind.

New tool to master in a week

One week sprint of familiarising myself with the events-based system: if/then conditional logic, global variables, and expression builders. Piece by piece, I replicated every flow. It felt less like prototyping and more like writing code with a visual interface. Surprisingly, I loved every minute of it.

117 bugs and counting

We ended up not having enough time to do user testing before the fiscal year launch deadline. So testing became something else entirely. Systematic, documented, thorough. I identified more than one hundred functional and UI bugs and logged each one into the Azure DevOps environment where the development team operated.

Documentation

Every screen documented. Every requirement communicated.

The Figma file was never going to be enough on its own. Every single screen was accompanied by a detailed specification.

Once the dev team had both the designs and specs, any remaining ambiguity was resolved through sessions with the front-end, back-end, and QA engineers. My job didn't end at handoff. It ended when the devs had everything they needed to build it right.

Reflection

Lessons I learned

Understand first. Design after.

I used to open Figma before I understood the problem. I had to break that habit consciously. The best design decisions I made happened while conceptual modelling in Mural, not in Figma. Weeks before a screen was ever touched.

Think in systems.

Good design at this scale isn't about individual screens. It's about understanding how everything connects: the laws, the data, the user, the overall system.
